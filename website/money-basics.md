# Money basics

You do not need to know accounting to run a household's money well. This page walks
through the handful of ideas that matter most, in the order most people find useful,
and shows where each one lives in BreadSched. It is general education, not financial
advice: your situation, your country's rules and your own priorities come first.

## Start with what you have

Make a list of every place money sits and every place it is owed: checking and
savings accounts, credit cards, a car loan, a mortgage, retirement accounts. That
list is the whole picture, and it is the first thing to put in BreadSched.

- What you **own** (bank balances, investments, a house at what it would sell for) are
  *assets*.
- What you **owe** (card balances, loans) are *debts*, or *liabilities*.
- Assets less debts is your **net worth**. Its level matters less than its direction:
  a net worth that rises a little most months is a household doing well.

You do not have to enter years of history. Start with today's balances, or import
the history you already have from your bank or another program and let BreadSched
pick up from there. See [Accounts](guide/overview.html#accounts) and
[Importing](guide/overview.html#import-and-gnucash-interoperability).

## Keep the record honest

A ledger is only useful if it matches the bank. Two habits keep it so:

1. **Enter or import every transaction**, including the small ones. Rules can sort
   imported transactions into categories for you.
2. **Reconcile** each statement: tick off what the statement shows, and the
   difference left over is the mistake to find. Once a month is plenty.

Every BreadSched transaction balances: money leaves one account and arrives in
another (spending "arrives" in an expense category such as Groceries). That is all
*double-entry* means, and it is why the balances can be trusted.

## Know your cash flow

Most money stress is not about *how much* but about *when*. Rent is due on the 1st
and the paycheck lands on the 5th; the car insurance comes once a year. A monthly
budget can balance on paper while the account runs dry for four days.

So BreadSched plans by **date**. Enter each bill and each paycheck on the day it
happens, and the Dashboard draws the cash you will have on hand each day ahead and
marks the lowest point. If the line dips below zero, you know before the bank does,
while there is still time to move a payment or some savings.

A small **cushion**, money in checking you never plan to spend, keeps an early bill
or a late paycheck from becoming an overdraft. The Dashboard's *Available* figure
already leaves out what is set aside for the bills due soon and for your savings
goals, so what it shows is yours to spend.

## Build an emergency fund

An emergency fund is money set aside for the job loss, the car repair, the broken
furnace: the things that are certain to happen to someone, sometime. A common
guideline is three to six months of essential outgoings (housing, utilities, food,
insurance, minimum debt payments), kept somewhere safe and easy to reach.

The Dashboard measures this for you: it adds up your committed monthly outgoings and
shows how many months your liquid money would cover. Start with one month; it is
already a large step.

## Budgeting with jars

Picture a row of jars on the kitchen shelf, one for each kind of spending. Each
payday you put a little in each jar; when you buy groceries, you take from the
Groceries jar. If a jar is empty, you know before you spend, not after. That is all a
budget is, and BreadSched builds one from things you have already entered:

- Every **scheduled bill** is a jar that must be full by its due date.
- Every **estimate** ("groceries, about 500 a month") is a jar for spending that does
  not arrive on a date. BreadSched can suggest estimates from your own history.
- Every **savings goal** is a jar you fill toward a target date.

Each income fills the jars it is meant for, by their share, and each actual
transaction draws from the jar it belongs to. The **Plan** shows planned against
actual for every month; **Budget jars** shows what each jar holds today.

Two kinds of jar deserve special mention:

- **Irregular bills.** The annual insurance premium or the twice-yearly property tax
  is easy to forget until it arrives. As a jar, it fills a little every paycheck, so
  the money is there when the bill is.
- **Spending that varies.** An estimate's jar empties at the end of its period:
  what you did not spend on groceries this month is not owed to groceries next month.
  Savings goals keep what is put in them.

See [Jars: how the pieces fit together](guide/overview.html#jars-how-the-pieces-fit-together).

## Credit cards

A credit card is a short loan on everything you buy with it. The habit that makes
cards cheap is simple: **pay the full statement balance by its due date** every
month, and treat a card purchase as money already spent. In BreadSched, a purchase on
the card is spending in its category the day you make it, and the card's payment is
a scheduled bill, so the Dashboard counts what you will owe.

If a balance is carried from month to month, it is a debt like any other, and
usually the most expensive one.

## Paying down debt

List each debt with its balance, its interest rate and its minimum payment. Pay every
minimum, then put anything extra toward one debt at a time. Two common orders:

- **Highest interest first** (the *avalanche*) costs the least in total.
- **Smallest balance first** (the *snowball*) clears whole debts sooner, which many
  people find keeps them going.

Either works far better than spreading extra payments thin. A **projection** can show
what an extra payment each month does to the payoff date and the interest paid. See
[Loans](guide/overview.html#create-a-loan) and
[Projection and scenarios](guide/overview.html#projection-and-scenarios).

## Look back to look ahead

Once a few months are recorded, the record starts to talk:

- **Spending by category** shows where the money went as a ring. The biggest slice is
  usually housing; the surprises are in the small ones.
- **Income and spending** puts each month's income beside its spending. What is left
  over, month after month, is your real capacity to save.
- **Net worth over time** shows the direction that matters most.

Every chart has its exact numbers beside it. See
[Reports and breadlets](guide/overview.html#reports-and-breadlets).

## Plan for years, not just months

A **projection** carries today's balances, your scheduled income and bills, your
estimates and your goals forward month by month, with assumptions such as raises,
inflation and investment returns that you choose. A **scenario** changes some of them
(a new job, a move, retiring two years earlier) so you can compare the two futures
side by side. A projection is a way to ask questions, not a promise: the assumptions
are yours, and a modest set is usually more useful than an optimistic one.

## A first month

1. Add your checking account and one credit card, with today's balances (or import
   their history).
2. Enter rent or mortgage, utilities and your paychecks as scheduled transactions.
3. Look at the Dashboard's cash-flow line. Is there a low point to plan around?
4. Add two or three estimates: groceries, fuel, eating out.
5. At month end, reconcile both accounts, then look at Spending by category.

That is enough to know more about your money than most households do. Add the rest
when it would help, and only then.
